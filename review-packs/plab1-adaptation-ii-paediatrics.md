## 1. PAN2628: Post-operative fever and distension in Hirschsprung disease

**Label:** 2026 UKMLA Adaptation II — adapted (Oct 2026)  
**Source ID:** N2628 (parked UN/UQ copy of the AKT question)  
**Difficulty:** Difficult  
**Domain:** Paediatrics

**Stem:** A 7-month-old boy had a pull-through operation for Hirschsprung disease two months ago and has since been well. Overnight he has become febrile and lethargic. His abdomen is tense and swollen and he is passing frequent, foul-smelling watery stools. He looks pale and mottled with a slow capillary refill.

Which diagnosis should be suspected first?

**Options:**

- A. **Enterocolitis complicating Hirschsprung disease**
- B. Self-limiting viral gastroenteritis acquired in the community
- C. Intussusception with early bowel ischaemia
- D. Recurrent Hirschsprung obstruction from retained aganglionic bowel
- E. Necrotising enterocolitis of prematurity

**Key:** A. Enterocolitis complicating Hirschsprung disease

**Why correct:** Enterocolitis is a serious bowel infection that can complicate Hirschsprung disease and can lead to sepsis. The combination of fever, abdominal distension and watery, foul-smelling diarrhoea in a child with Hirschsprung disease should be treated as enterocolitis until proven otherwise, and the shock features here make this an emergency. He needs emergency hospital assessment with sepsis management and antibiotics. Source: NHS.uk, Hirschsprung's disease, page last reviewed January 2023.

**Why others are wrong:** B. Gastroenteritis is common, but in a child with Hirschsprung disease these features must not be assumed to be a simple viral illness because delay risks sepsis. C. Intussusception causes colicky pain with drawing up of the legs and redcurrant-jelly stool rather than fever with profuse watery diarrhoea. D. Retained aganglionic bowel causes constipation and obstructive symptoms over time, not an abrupt febrile illness with shock and frequent watery foul-smelling stools after a period of wellness. E. Necrotising enterocolitis affects premature neonates in the first weeks of life, not a 7-month-old.

**Citation:** NHS.uk, Hirschsprung's disease, page last reviewed January 2023.


## 2. PAN2964: Cyanosis unresponsive to oxygen in a newborn

**Label:** 2026 UKMLA Adaptation II — adapted (Oct 2026)  
**Source ID:** N2964 (parked UN/UQ copy of the AKT question)  
**Difficulty:** Difficult  
**Domain:** Paediatrics

**Stem:** A term baby is noticed by the midwife to have blue lips and tongue at 2 hours of age. He feeds poorly but is not working hard to breathe, the chest is clear and no murmur is heard. His oxygen saturation stays low despite oxygen by face mask, and the chest X-ray shows normal lung fields.

Which cardiac diagnosis is most likely?

**Options:**

- A. Isolated patent ductus arteriosus
- B. **Transposition of the great arteries**
- C. Small perimembranous ventricular septal defect
- D. Secundum atrial septal defect
- E. Coarctation of the aorta

**Key:** B. Transposition of the great arteries

**Why correct:** In transposition of the great arteries the aorta arises from the right ventricle and the pulmonary artery from the left, so the pulmonary and systemic circulations run in parallel and little oxygenated blood reaches the body. Babies are cyanosed from the first hours or days with little respiratory distress, often no murmur, and cyanosis that does not correct with oxygen. This is a medical emergency needing urgent paediatric cardiology input. Source: British Heart Foundation, Transposition of the great arteries; NHS.uk, Congenital heart disease, page last reviewed December 2025.

**Why others are wrong:** A. An isolated patent ductus is a left-to-right shunt and does not cause profound cyanosis in a term baby. C. A small ventricular septal defect is a left-to-right shunt that usually presents later with a murmur or heart failure. D. A secundum atrial septal defect is a left-to-right shunt and rarely causes problems in the newborn period. E. Coarctation presents with weak femoral pulses or circulatory collapse, not isolated cyanosis with clear lungs.

**Citation:** British Heart Foundation, Transposition of the great arteries; NHS.uk, Congenital heart disease, page last reviewed December 2025.


## 3. PAN2979: Bleeding gums and painful legs in a child on a restricted diet

**Label:** 2026 UKMLA Adaptation II — adapted (Oct 2026)  
**Source ID:** N2979 (parked UN/UQ copy of the AKT question)  
**Difficulty:** Moderate  
**Domain:** Paediatrics

**Stem:** A 3-year-old boy with autism spectrum disorder has for many months eaten only plain pasta, white bread and crisps, refusing all fruit and vegetables. His parents bring him because he is miserable, will not walk and cries when his legs are touched. He has swollen, spongy gums that bleed easily and tiny red spots around hair follicles on his shins. His platelet count and clotting screen are normal.

Which deficiency is most likely?

**Options:**

- A. **Vitamin C deficiency**
- B. Vitamin K deficiency
- C. Vitamin D deficiency
- D. Vitamin A deficiency
- E. Vitamin B12 deficiency

**Key:** A. Vitamin C deficiency

**Why correct:** Vitamin C is needed for collagen formation, and deficiency (scurvy) causes swollen bleeding gums, skin spots, easy bruising and joint or leg pain. Children who find it hard to have a healthy diet, for example because of very restricted eating, are at risk. A normal platelet count and clotting screen argue against a primary bleeding disorder or vitamin K deficiency. Treatment is vitamin C replacement with dietary advice. Source: NHS.uk, Scurvy, page last reviewed September 2023.

**Why others are wrong:** B. Vitamin K deficiency causes bleeding through a clotting defect, which is excluded by the normal clotting screen. C. Vitamin D deficiency causes rickets with bone changes and bone pain but not gum bleeding and perifollicular spots. D. Vitamin A deficiency causes night blindness and dry eyes. E. Vitamin B12 deficiency causes macrocytic anaemia and neurological features rather than gum bleeding.

**Citation:** NHS.uk, Scurvy, page last reviewed September 2023.


## 4. PAN2980: Vitamin K prophylaxis counselling

**Label:** 2026 UKMLA Adaptation II — adapted (Oct 2026)  
**Source ID:** N2980 (parked UN/UQ copy of the AKT question)  
**Difficulty:** Easy  
**Domain:** Paediatrics

**Stem:** A woman at 38 weeks of an uncomplicated first pregnancy plans to breastfeed. She asks the midwife why her baby will be offered an injection soon after birth, and says a friend told her it is only needed for babies who are born early or become bruised.

What is the most appropriate advice about vitamin K?

**Options:**

- A. Vitamin K is needed only for premature babies or those with visible bruising
- B. Breast milk contains enough vitamin K, so prophylaxis is unnecessary
- C. **Offer vitamin K to every newborn, preferably as an intramuscular injection**
- D. Vitamin K should be given only once a baby has started to bleed
- E. Oral vitamin K is the only route recommended in the UK for newborns

**Key:** C. Offer vitamin K to every newborn, preferably as an intramuscular injection

**Why correct:** Vitamin K is offered to all newborn babies to prevent vitamin K deficiency bleeding, a rare but serious and sometimes fatal disorder. NICE advises an intramuscular injection because this works best, with oral vitamin K as an alternative if parents decline, though oral dosing may need to be repeated after discharge. Source: NICE NG235, Intrapartum care, 2023 (amended 2026).

**Why others are wrong:** A. Newborns of any gestation have low vitamin K stores, so prophylaxis is offered to all. B. Breast milk is a relatively poor source of vitamin K, so exclusively breastfed babies remain at risk. D. Prophylaxis aims to prevent the first bleed, which can be catastrophic such as an intracranial haemorrhage. E. The intramuscular injection is the recommended route, with oral vitamin K only an alternative.

**Citation:** NICE NG235, Intrapartum care, 2023 (amended 2026).


## 5. PAN2981: Pallor in a toddler on a milk-dominated diet

**Label:** 2026 UKMLA Adaptation II — adapted (Oct 2026)  
**Source ID:** N2981 (parked UN/UQ copy of the AKT question)  
**Difficulty:** Easy  
**Domain:** Paediatrics

**Stem:** A 20-month-old girl is brought by her father because she looks pale and tired. She drinks a very large volume of cow's milk from a bottle each day and eats little meat, fish or fortified cereal. Her growth is normal and there is no history of bleeding or jaundice. A blood count shows a low haemoglobin with small red cells.

What is the most likely diagnosis?

**Options:**

- A. Vitamin B12 deficiency
- B. Folate deficiency
- C. Beta-thalassaemia trait
- D. **Iron deficiency anaemia**
- E. Anaemia of chronic disease

**Key:** D. Iron deficiency anaemia

**Why correct:** A diet dominated by milk displaces iron-rich foods, and milk and dairy in large amounts can also make it harder for the body to absorb iron, so this is a classic route to iron deficiency in toddlers, which causes a microcytic, hypochromic anaemia. Poor dietary intake is the first thing to explore, and dietary advice with iron replacement is the usual treatment. Source: NICE CKS, Anaemia - iron deficiency, last revised July 2026; NHS.uk, Iron deficiency anaemia, page last reviewed January 2024.

**Why others are wrong:** A. Vitamin B12 deficiency causes a macrocytic anaemia and is not caused by a milk-heavy diet. B. Folate deficiency also causes a macrocytic anaemia. C. Beta-thalassaemia trait causes a microcytic picture but does not explain the dietary history and usually produces a mild, lifelong anaemia with a normal or raised red cell count. E. Anaemia of chronic disease needs an underlying inflammatory illness, which is absent here.

**Citation:** NICE CKS, Anaemia - iron deficiency, last revised July 2026; NHS.uk, Iron deficiency anaemia, page last reviewed January 2024.


## 6. PAN3330: Jerky movements after a sore throat

**Label:** 2026 UKMLA Adaptation II — adapted (Oct 2026)  
**Source ID:** N3330 (parked UN/UQ copy of the AKT question)  
**Difficulty:** Moderate  
**Domain:** Paediatrics

**Stem:** A 9-year-old girl is brought to her GP because over a fortnight she has become clumsy, drops her pencil and spills drinks. Her teacher has noticed grimacing and brief, irregular jerky movements of her hands and face that disappear when she sleeps, and she has been unusually tearful. Five weeks ago she had a sore throat that was not treated. She has no fever, joint pain or rash.

Which is the most likely cause of her movements?

**Options:**

- A. Tic disorder (Tourette syndrome)
- B. Wilson disease
- C. Functional movement disorder
- D. Focal motor seizures
- E. **Sydenham chorea**

**Key:** E. Sydenham chorea

**Why correct:** Sydenham chorea is a neurological manifestation of acute rheumatic fever, appearing some weeks after a group A streptococcal throat infection. It causes rapid, purposeless movements of the face and limbs, clumsiness and emotional lability, and may be the only feature. She needs paediatric assessment including a heart check for carditis and measures to prevent recurrence. Source: NHS.uk, Rheumatic fever, page last reviewed January 2025.

**Why others are wrong:** A. Tics are stereotyped, can be briefly suppressed and are preceded by an urge, rather than causing new clumsiness and spilling things. B. Wilson disease is rare at this age and presents with liver disease or dystonia and tremor rather than chorea after infection. C. Functional movements vary with distraction and do not follow an untreated streptococcal infection in this way. D. Focal seizures cause stereotyped episodes, not continuous movements that vanish in sleep.

**Citation:** NHS.uk, Rheumatic fever, page last reviewed January 2025.


## 7. PAN3422: Speaks at home but not at school

**Label:** 2026 UKMLA Adaptation II — adapted (Oct 2026)  
**Source ID:** N3422 (parked UN/UQ copy of the AKT question)  
**Difficulty:** Moderate  
**Domain:** Paediatrics

**Stem:** A 6-year-old girl talks freely and confidently at home, but since starting school a year ago has not spoken to her teachers or classmates, although she follows instructions, writes well and sometimes nods or points. Her parents say she also goes quiet and still when relatives she rarely sees visit. Her hearing is normal and her language development has otherwise been typical.

Which is the most likely diagnosis?

**Options:**

- A. Autism spectrum disorder
- B. Developmental language disorder
- C. Oppositional defiant disorder
- D. **Selective mutism, an anxiety disorder**
- E. Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder

**Key:** D. Selective mutism, an anxiety disorder

**Why correct:** Selective mutism is an anxiety disorder in which a child is unable to speak in particular social situations, such as with classmates at school or relatives they rarely see, while speaking normally elsewhere. It most often starts in early childhood and is often first noticed when the child begins to interact with people outside the family, and the child is not choosing to be defiant. Early recognition allows a supportive, anxiety-focused approach. Source: NHS.uk, Selective mutism, page last reviewed February 2023.

**Why others are wrong:** A. Autism involves wider difficulties in social communication and restricted, repetitive behaviours across settings, not silence limited to certain people. B. Developmental language disorder affects language ability in every setting, whereas she speaks fluently at home. C. Oppositional defiant disorder involves persistent defiant behaviour, not a freeze response when expected to speak. E. ADHD causes inattention and impulsivity rather than selective absence of speech.

**Citation:** NHS.uk, Selective mutism, page last reviewed February 2023.

## 9. PAN4179: Painless flat feet in a young child

**Label:** 2026 UKMLA Adaptation II — adapted (Oct 2026)  
**Source ID:** N4179 (parked UN/UQ copy of the AKT question)  
**Difficulty:** Easy  
**Domain:** Paediatrics

**Stem:** A grandmother notices that her 4-year-old granddaughter seems to have 'collapsed arches' when she stands. The child runs and plays like her peers and has never complained of foot or leg pain. Her arches reappear when she stands on tiptoe, her gait is normal and the rest of the examination is unremarkable.

What is the most appropriate advice for her parents?

**Options:**

- A. Arrange weight-bearing foot X-rays to look for an abnormal bony bar between the tarsal bones
- B. Refer for custom-made insoles so that the arches can be rebuilt over time
- C. **Explain that this is a common finding and no treatment is needed at present**
- D. Request nerve conduction studies to look for an underlying neuromuscular disorder
- E. Advise night-time foot splinting for several months to correct the shape

**Key:** C. Explain that this is a common finding and no treatment is needed at present

**Why correct:** Flexible flat feet are very common in young children, are usually painless and do not limit activity. When the arch reappears on tiptoe and the child is well, no treatment is needed, and the parents can be reassured. Pain, stiffness, a rigid foot, a single affected foot or problems with walking or balance would need further assessment. Source: NHS.uk, Flat feet, page last reviewed June 2025.

**Why others are wrong:** A. A tarsal coalition gives a rigid, often painful foot, which does not match this flexible painless foot. B. Insoles do not change the shape of the foot and are not needed when there are no symptoms. D. A neuromuscular disorder would cause weakness or abnormal gait, which she does not have. E. Splinting has no role in a painless flexible flat foot.

**Citation:** NHS.uk, Flat feet, page last reviewed June 2025.


## 10. PAN4202: Bedwetting with constipation

**Label:** 2026 UKMLA Adaptation II — adapted (Oct 2026)  
**Source ID:** N4202 (parked UN/UQ copy of the AKT question)  
**Difficulty:** Moderate  
**Domain:** Paediatrics

**Stem:** A 6-year-old girl wets the bed on most nights but is reliably dry in the daytime. Her mother says she opens her bowels only about twice a week, passes hard stools and has occasional smears of loose stool in her underwear. Examination is normal.

What is the most appropriate approach to her management?

**Options:**

- A. Start an enuresis alarm now and leave the bowel problem until a later review
- B. **Identify and treat the constipation as part of the bedwetting plan**
- C. Prescribe desmopressin to be taken every night starting straight away
- D. Arrange an urgent ultrasound scan of the kidneys and bladder
- E. Reassure the family that she will outgrow both problems without help

**Key:** B. Identify and treat the constipation as part of the bedwetting plan

**Why correct:** Constipation is a common contributor to bedwetting, and the smearing here suggests faecal loading with overflow. NICE advises that children with bedwetting and soiling or constipation are investigated and treated for the bowel problem in line with its constipation guidance, as the bedwetting may improve once this is dealt with. Source: NICE CG111, Bedwetting in under 19s, 2010; NICE CKS, Constipation in children, last revised August 2025.

**Why others are wrong:** A. Starting bedwetting treatment while ignoring the constipation misses a recognised cause and is less likely to work. C. Desmopressin does not address the underlying bowel problem and should not be the immediate step. D. Imaging is not needed for bedwetting with an obvious cause and a normal examination. E. Constipation with soiling should be treated rather than left to resolve.

**Citation:** NICE CG111, Bedwetting in under 19s, 2010; NICE CKS, Constipation in children, last revised August 2025.

**Label:** 2026 UKMLA Adaptation II — adapted (Oct 2026)  
**Source ID:** N4224 (parked UN/UQ copy of the AKT question)  
**Difficulty:** Easy  
**Domain:** Paediatrics

**Stem:** A term baby boy born by uncomplicated vaginal delivery to a mother without diabetes fed eagerly for the first day. At 20 hours of age the midwife finds him drowsy, taking only a few sucks at the breast and floppy when handled. His temperature is normal.

**Options:**

**Key:** E. Measure a capillary blood glucose level straight away

## 12. PAN4226: Ongoing oxygen need after prematurity

**Label:** 2026 UKMLA Adaptation II — adapted (Oct 2026)  
**Source ID:** N4226 (parked UN/UQ copy of the AKT question)  
**Difficulty:** Moderate  
**Domain:** Paediatrics

**Stem:** A boy born at 27 weeks' gestation needed several weeks of mechanical ventilation and extra oxygen on the neonatal unit. By the time he reaches his original due date he still needs low-flow oxygen, with mild tachypnoea and subcostal recession, and his chest X-ray shows persistent hazy lung fields.

What is the most likely diagnosis?

**Options:**

- A. Neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (surfactant deficiency)
- B. **Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD)**
- C. Transient tachypnoea of the newborn
- D. Congenital lobar emphysema
- E. Meconium aspiration syndrome

**Key:** B. Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD)

**Why correct:** A very preterm baby who needed prolonged ventilation and oxygen and still has an oxygen requirement and abnormal lung fields around the expected date of birth has bronchopulmonary dysplasia, the chronic lung disease of prematurity. Immature lungs injured by ventilation, oxygen and inflammation heal abnormally, leaving a continuing oxygen need. Source: NICE NG124, Specialist neonatal respiratory care for babies born preterm, 2019.

**Why others are wrong:** A. Respiratory distress syndrome is the acute surfactant-deficiency illness of the first days of life, not an oxygen need persisting for weeks. C. Transient tachypnoea of the newborn settles within a day or two and does not follow weeks of ventilation. D. Congenital lobar emphysema is a developmental overinflation of one lobe, unrelated to ventilator injury in prematurity. E. Meconium aspiration affects term or post-term babies who pass meconium before birth, and it is not the consequence of extreme prematurity.

**Citation:** NICE NG124, Specialist neonatal respiratory care for babies born preterm, 2019.


## 13. PAN4233: Fever, cough and fast breathing in a child

**Label:** 2026 UKMLA Adaptation II — adapted (Oct 2026)  
**Source ID:** N4233 (parked UN/UQ copy of the AKT question)  
**Difficulty:** Easy  
**Domain:** Paediatrics

**Stem:** A 6-year-old girl has had three days of fever and cough. She is breathing faster than expected for her age and has a sharp pain on the right side of her chest when she breathes in deeply. On examination, air entry is reduced and there are coarse crackles at the right base. The rest of the chest is clear and there is no wheeze.

What is the most likely diagnosis?

- B. Viral upper respiratory tract infection
- C. Whooping cough (pertussis)
- D. **Community-acquired pneumonia**

**Key:** D. Community-acquired pneumonia

**Why correct:** Persistent fever, cough, fast breathing and pleuritic pain with focal crackles and reduced air entry at one lung base point to a lower respiratory tract infection of the lung tissue, which is community-acquired pneumonia. Whooping cough causes paroxysms of coughing with whoops or vomiting and usually little fever. E. Bronchiolitis is a diffuse illness of infants under a year old, with widespread crackles and wheeze rather than focal signs in a school-age child.

**Citation:** NICE CKS, Cough - acute with chest signs in children, last revised September 2025.

## 36. PAN10054: Patent ductus arteriosus: continuous murmur in a preterm baby
